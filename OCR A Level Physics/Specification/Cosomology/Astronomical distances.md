# Units of distance
As the distances on the astronomical scale are so vast, we use specialist units in order of increasing magnitude.

# Astronomical Unit (AU)
An astronomical unit is the average distance from the Earth to the Sun. One astronomical unit is approximately $1.5\times 10^{11}m$.

We use AU to express the average distance between the distance between the Sun and other planets in the Solar System. 

# The light-year (ly)
A light year is the distance travelled by light in a vacuum in a time of one year. This distance is approximately $9.46\times 10^{15}$. 

The light-year is often used when expressing distances to other stars or other galaxies

# The parsec
The parsec is defined as the distance at which a radius of AU subtends an angle of one arc second.

One parsec is determined by using simple trigonometry:
$$
\tan(1 \ \text{arcsecond}) = \frac{1AU}{1pc} \implies 1 pc = \frac{1.50\times 10 ^{11}}{\tan\left( \frac{1}{3600} \right)} = 3.1\times 10 ^{16}
$$
Knowing the small angle approximation of $\tan\theta$ is $\tan\theta \approx\theta$m for very small angles like 1 arc second, if the point s is at a distance $S$ is a distance of $d$ parsecs, then the angle subtended is simply $\frac{1}{d}$ arcseconds. This is because the numerator of angle is kept constant at $1AU$. 

# Stellar parallax
Stellar parallax can be used to determine the distance to stars less than 100pc from Earth.

Parallax is the apparent shift in the position of a relativey close star against the backdrop of much more distance stars as the Earth orbits the Sun.

When the Eartph is in a position around the sun in the Month of June. The star appear to be in a first position. 6 months later in December, the star appears in position 2.

With precise measures of the angle between the different images of the star, the distance can be determined using:
$$
d=\frac{1}{p}
$$
Where $d$ is in parsecs and $p$ is in arcseconds.
