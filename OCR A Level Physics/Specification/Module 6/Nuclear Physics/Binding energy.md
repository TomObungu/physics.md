## Deuterium nucleus 
Deuterium is an isotope of hydrogen. A nucleus of deuterium consists of one proton and an one neutron. All nucleons are bound together by the strong nuclear force and are separated by doing work supplied externally. 

According to Einstein's mass-energy equation, energy and mass are equivalent, therefore the total mass of the separated nucleons must be greater than the mass of the deuterium nucleus. 

In terms of unified atomic mass units $u$ ($1u = 1.661 \times 10^{-27}kg$), a deuterium nucleus has.  mass $2.01335u$ a proton has mass $1.007276u$ and neutron has mass $1.008665u$. The total mass of the separated proton and neutron is more than the deuterium nucleus. This difference of $0.002388u$ is equivalent to energy of about $3.5 \times 10 ^{-13}J$ or $2.2MeV$. In simple terms, a minimum energy of about $2.2MeV$ is needed to completely separate the nucleons of a deuterium nucleus. 

The reverse of the process of constructing a deuterium nucleus would mean energy of $2.2MeV$ would be released - most likely in the form of a photon. 

# Mass defect and binding energy
In the deuterium nucleus above, the difference in mass of $0.002388u$ is known as the mass defect of the deuterium nucleus. 

The mass defect is defined as the difference between the mass of the completely separated nucleons and the mass of nucleus. 

The energy difference of $2.2MeV$ for the deuterium nucleus is known as its binding energy. 

The binding energy of a nucleus is defined as the minimum energy required to completely separate a nucleus into its constituent protons an neutrons 

To calculate the binding energy of a nucleus, you can use Einstein's mass-energy equation:

$$
\text{binding energy of a nucleus} = \text{mass defect of necleus} \times c^{2}
$$

Remember that binding energy holds the **nuclei** together and not the **atom**.

The binding energy is not the same for all nuclei. A uranium-235 has 92 protons and 143 neutrons. The external external energy required to split this nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons to be much greater than $2.2MeV$. There are many more strong nuclear bonds to be broken. 

## Worked Example 
The mass of a unranium-235 $^{235}_{92}U$ nucleus is $235.004393u$ Calculate its binding energy. 

1. Calculating the total mass of the constituent nucleons 
Uranium has $92$ protons and and $(235-92)=143$ neutrons. Therefore the mass of the nucleons is:
$$
= 92(1.007276) + 143(1.008665) = 236.908487u
$$
2. Calculate the mass defect of the uranium-353 nucleus. 
$$
\text{mass defect} = 235.004393 - 236.908487 = -1.904094u
$$
$1u = 1.661 \times 10^{-27}kg$, therefore the mass defect can be found in kg:
$$
= 1.90409(1.661 \times 10^{-27}) = 3.162\dots \times 10^{-27}kg
$$
3. Using Einstein's mass-energy equation to calculate binding energy:
4. $$
5. \begin{gather*}
\text{binding energy} = (3.162\times 10^{-27})( 3 \times 10^{8})^{2} = 2.84 \times 10^{-10J} \\ \\
= \frac{2.84 \times 10^{-10}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 1780MeV
\end{gather*}
$$

# Binding energy per nucleon
It is possible to determine the average binding energy per nucleon of nuclei. The greater the binding energy (BE) per nucleon, the more tightly bound are the nucleons within the nucleus or in other words a nucleus is more stable if it has a greater BE per nucleon. 

The binding energy per nucleon for uranium-235 is $1780MeV$. There are 235 nucleons in uranium 235 thus the average binding energy is $1780 / 235 \approx 7.6MeV$. For deuterium  it  is $2.2MeV /2 \approx 1.1MeV$.

- Nuclei with atomic number, $A$, $A<56$, the BE per nucleon increases as A increases 

- For $A > 56$, the BE per nucleon decreases as $A$ increases.

- The nucleus of iron-56 $^{56}_{26}Fe$ has the greatest BE per nucleon - It is the most stable isotope in nature. 

- The $^{4}_{2}He$, helium-4 alpha particle has abnormally BE per nucleon than its immediate neighbours. The same goes for carbon-12 and oxygen-16 nuclei. 

- Energy is released in natural radioactive decay. In spontaneous decay, the total binding energy of the parent nucleus is less than the binding energy of the daughter nucleus and the alpha particle. This difference is released as kinetic energy in decay. 

- In fusion, two low number $A$ nuclei join together to produce a higher $A$ number nucleus. The newly formed nucleus has much greater binding energy than the initial nuclei and therefore energy is released. 

- In fission, a high $A$ nucleus produces two lower $A$ nuclei. Energy is released because the two nuclei produced have higher binding energy than the parent nuclei. 

The binding energy per nucleon can be given by:
$$
\text{Binding energy per nucleon } = \frac{\text{mass defect}}{\text{nucleon number}}
$$

The mass defect as stated earlier is given by taking the atomic mass of the nucleus on its own in atomic units, finding the mass of the sum of the individual protons and neutrons and finding this differences. 
$$
\Delta m =\text{mass defect} = \left(\sum\text{individual protons and neutrons}\right) -\text{mass of nucleus} 
$$
Recall from Einstien's equation:
$$
E = \Delta mc^{2}
$$
Thus:
$$
\text{B.E.P.N} = \frac{\Delta mc^{2}}{A}
$$
Where is the nucleon number of the atom.
