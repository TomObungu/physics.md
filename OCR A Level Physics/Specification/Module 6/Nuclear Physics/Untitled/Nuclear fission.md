In 1938, physicists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann observed traces of lighter elements in a sample of Uranium-235 being irradiated by slow neutrons.  

In each fission reaction, energy is released as per Einstein's mass-energy equation.  

A kilogram of Uranium-235 can produce millions of times more energy than a kilogram of coal. 

# Induced fission
Uranium mined from ore consist of about 99.3% uranium-238 isotope and uranium-235. Uranium-235 easily undergoes fission on absorbing a slow neutron. These neutrons are known as thermal neutrons. Uranium-238 nuclei are more likely to absorb the neutrons that  undergo fission. 

However both uranium-235 and uranium-238 can split spontaneously without absorbing neutrons. Uranium-236 has a much greater chance to split spontaneously. Below is typical fission reaction of uranium-235 by a thermal neutron:
$$
^{1}_{0}n+ ^{235}_{92}U \to ^{236}_{92}U \to ^{141}_{56}Ba + ^{92}_{36}Ke + 3 ^{1}_{0}n
$$

The uranium-235 nucleus captures the thermal neutron and becomes an unstable uranium-236 nucleus. Microseconds later, the uranium-236 splits and the daughter nuclei are produced. Three fast moving neutrons are also produced. The equation remains balanced with the total number of protons and neutrons being conserved of 92 and 236.

# Fission energy
The total mass of particle after the fission reaction is always less than the total mass of the particle before the reaction. This difference in mass is directly converted in energy as per $\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}$. 

The total binding energy of the particle after fission is greater than that of before. We are left with two elements and 3 substituent. The total binding energy of those elements is greater than of the single uranium-235 nuclei. 

The difference in the binding energies is equal to the energy released. 

The energy released in a single fission reaction is a combination of the kinetic energy of the particles produced and the energy of photons and neutrinos emitted. 

## Worked Example
The difference in mass $\Delta m$ in the induced fission of uranium-235 by a slowing neutron to barium-141 and krypton-92, is about $0.185u$. Calculate the total energy released in this reaction in $MeV$. 

1. Finding $0.185u$ in kg:
$$
0.185u = 0.185(1.661 \times 10^{-27}) = 3.07 \times 10^{-28}kg
$$
2. Convert it to energy using $\Delta E = \Delta mc^{2}$:
$$
\begin{gather*}
\Delta E = (3.07 \times 10^{-28})(3 \times 10^{8})^{2} = 2.76 \times 10^{-11}
\end{gather*}
$$
3. Convert to $eV$
$$
\frac{2.76 \times 10^{-11}}{1.60 \times 10^{-19}} = 172.5 \times 10^{-6} eV = 170MeV
$$

# Chain reaction
The fission of uranium-235 is more likely with slow neutrons than fast.

If the three fast neutrons produced in a fission reaction reaction can be slowed, a chain reaction becomes possible - These three neutrons start three more chain reactions. After $n$ fission events $3^{n}$ would be present and the rate of energy release will be exponential with time. 

Nuclear reactors in power plants ensure a steady product of power by ensuring only one slow neutron survives between successive fission reactions.

# Inside a fission reactor
Fuel rods inside a fission reactor contain the radioactive material. A coolant is used to remove the thermal energy produced from the fission reaction within the fissile fuel. The fuel rods are surrounded by the moderator and the control rods can be moved in and our of the core. 

## Fuel rods
Fuel rods contain enriched uranium consisting of mainly uranium-238 with 2-3% uranium-235

## Moderator
The role of the moderator is the slow down the fast neutrons produced in fission reactions. The material of moderator must be cheap and readily available and must not absorb the neutrons in the reactor. 

Induced fission of uranium-235 produced fast neutrons with kinetic energies of up to 1 $MeV$. These neutrons have a low chance of being absorbed by surrounding nuclei.  

Thermal neutrons have a mean kinetic energy of $\frac{3}{2}kT$, where $k$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is the thermodynamic temperature of the reactor core. Their root mean squared is just a few $kms ^{-1}$.

Fast moving neutrons bounce of initial uranium nuclei with negligible loss of kinetic energy. If the neutrons collide elastically with protons with water of carbon, they transfer kinetic energy and slow. Therefore water and carbon are good candidates for a moderator. 

In many cases the moderator is also the coolant.

# Control rods
Control rods are made of material whose nuclei readily absorb neutrons. This is most commonly boron or cadmium. The position of the control rods is automatically adjust to ensure that exactly one slow neutron survives per fission reaction. The reaction can be completely stopped by pushing the control rods into the reactor core. 

# Radioactive waste and environmental impact
Neutrons of immediate kinetic energy from fission of uranium-235 are absorbed by surrounding uranium-238. The nuclei of uranium-238 quickly decay into the nuclei of plutonium-239.:
$$
^{238}_{92}U + ^{1}_{0}n \to ^{239}_{92}U \to \underbrace{ ^{239}_{92} Np }_{ \underbrace{ \beta^{-} \text{ decay}  }_{ t_{\frac{1}{2} = 24 \text{mins}} }} \to\underbrace{  ^{239}_{94}Pu }_{ \underbrace{ \beta^{-} \text{decay} }_{ t_{\frac{1}{2} = 2.4 \text{days}} } }
$$
Plutonium-239 is extremely hazardous, toxic and radioactive. It also has a half life of 24 thousand years. 

Radioactive waste from nuclear reactors cannot be disposed as normal waste. High-level radioactive waste, including spent fuel rods, must be burred deep underground for many centuries because isotopes with long half-lives must not enter our water and food supplies. 

This presents us with practical and ethical issues. The burial locations need to geologically stable, secure from and designed for safety. 

# Capture Cross Sections
The interaction of neutrons with nuclei inside a nuclear reaction depends on their speed. The idea of 'cross-section' is used to indicate the chance of neutron being absorbed by a specific nucleus. 

Imagine the nucleus as a disc that will absorb the neutron if the neutron hits this disk. The cross sectional area if measured in $m^{2}$, or barns. 1 barn $= 10^{-28}m^{2}$.
