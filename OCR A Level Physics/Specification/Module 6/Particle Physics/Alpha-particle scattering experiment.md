	Before 1911 physicists knew about atoms and also knew about their diameter to be about $10^{-10}m$. Then experiments carried out by Rutherford, Geiger and Marsden on the scattering of alpha particles by thin metal foils provided evidence for the nuclear model of the atom. 

# Rutherford's scattering experiment
Figure 1 shows the arrangement used to perform the scattering experiments. A narrow beam of alpha particles, all of the same kinetic energy, from a radioactive source were targeted towards a gold foil only a few atoms thick.

The particles were scattered by the gold foil and detected on a zinc sulfide screen screen mounted in front of a microscope. Each alpha particle hitting this fluorescent screen would produce a tiny speck of light.  

Th microscope was moved around in order to count the number of alpha particles scattered through different values of the angle $\theta$ per minute. From $0$ to $180^\circ$

## Observations
The experiment lead to the two following observations that could not support Thompson's plum pudding model of the atom:

- Most of the alpha particles went straight through the gold foil. This meant that the gold atoms were mostly empty space
- Some of the alpha particles were scattered through large angles. This meant that each gold atom has a positive nuclear with a radius less than $10^{-14}$

An alpha particle is a helium nucleus with charge of $+2e$. The charge on the gold nucleus is $+79e$. The scattering of an alpha particle can be explained in terms of electrostatic repulsion by the gold nucleus. 

The force on the particle can be calculated using Coulomb's law:
$$
F = \frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r^{2}}
