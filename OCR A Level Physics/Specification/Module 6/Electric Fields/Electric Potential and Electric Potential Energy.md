# Force-distance graph for point and spherical charges
For a uniformly charged sphere, it can be treated a point and be modelled with $F=\frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r^{2}}$, where $r$ also considers the distance from the centre of the sphere. 

For a graph of $F$ against separation $r$, the area under the graph is equal to the work done. This is from definition of work done being force multiplied by distance ($W=Fd$)

![[Pasted image 20251211010524.png]]

Much like gravitational potential energy, the total work done to bring the particles from infinity to a separation $r$ is the total area under the graph in the above diagram of force against separation. 

Showing the area under graph to be gives us an equation for work:
$$
\begin{gather*}
\text{Area} = Fr \\ \\
= -\frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r^{2}}r \\ \\
= \frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r} 
\end{gather*}
$$
Thus the total work done is the same as electric potential energy $E$:
$$
E = \frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}
$$
The letter $E$ is used for both electric field strength and electric potential energy.

When pushing two positively charged particles close to each other, the resistance you feel as they become closer is a gain of electric potential energy. 

When stopping pushing , the positively charged particles will repel each other and electric potential energy will be converted into kinetic energy 

If one of the particles has a negative charge, then the value for $E$ will also be negative. The force between the particles will be attractive. 

The magnitude of $E$ represents the external energy required to completely separate the charged particles to infinity. 

# Electric potential
The electric potential $V$ at a point is defined as the work done per unit charge in bringing a positive test charge from infinity to that point. 

If the test charge is $q$, the equation for $V$ can be determined by dividing electric potential energy $E$ by $q$. Therefore:
$$
V = \frac{E}{q} = \frac{Q\cancel{ q }}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r\cancel{ q }} =\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}
$$
The unit for electrical potential is $JC ^{-1}$ or **volts *$V$**

Notice that, voltage is electric potential difference. Potential difference is the work done per unit charge between two points around the particle of charge $Q$.

![[Pasted image 20251211033105.png]]

The electric p.d between points $A$ and $B$ in figure 4 is the differences in the values of electric potential at these points. 

Thus if you were to place a negative test charge $q$ in the field and move it from $A$ to $B$, the change in electric potential between $A$ and $B$ multiplied by $q$ will give the work done and thus the electric potential energy.

Remember that negative values of electric potential denote an attractive field. In this case, it is a between a positive and negative charge thus the values of $q$ will be negative.
$$
\begin{gather*}
\Delta V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\left( -\frac{1}{r_{B}}--\frac{1}{r_{A}}  \right) \\ \\
= \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\left( \frac{1}{r_{A}}-\frac{1}{r_{B}}  \right) \\ \\
\end{gather*}
$$
Thus this will give you the potential difference $V$. Now in order to get work from $V$, recall that $V$ is work done per unit charge $V=\frac{W}{Q}$ thus multiplying the by test negative charge $q$ will give you the work required to prove a test charge $q$ from $A$ to $B$ when in the electric field of a positive point charge:
$$
W=Vq=\Delta Vq
$$
## In relation to capacitance
A capacitor stores charge. An isolated charged sphere or radius $R$ also stores charge.  Since it stores charge, it can be treated as a capacitor as well. This is is because itself has some charge. 

The capacitance $C$ of a charged sphere with radius $R$, is the ratio of the charge it stores, $Q$ to the electric potential $V$ at its surface:

$$
\begin{gather*}
V = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}R}
\\ \\ 
C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}R}{\cancel{ Q }}\cancel{ Q } = 4\pi \varepsilon_{0}R
\end{gather*}
$$
