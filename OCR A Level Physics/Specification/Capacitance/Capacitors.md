Capacitors are electrical components in which charge is separated. A capacitor consists of two metallic plates separated from each other by an insulator, often known as a dielectric, such as air paper, ceramic or mica (natural silicate insulator). 

## Storing charge
When a capacitor is connected to the cell, electrons flow for a short time and cause a brief current. The electrons cannot travel between the plates because of insulation. This very brief current means electrons are removed from plate A and deposited at plate B. Plate A gains a positive charge and plate B acquires a negative charge.  

As current must be the same at all points and charge must be conserved, the two plates have an equal but opposite charge of magnitude $Q$. Therefore there is a difference in potential across the plates. 

Current in the circuit falls to zero when the p.d across the the plates is equal to the e.m.f $\varepsilon$ of the cell. In this case, the capacitor is fully charged. The net charge on the capacitor plates is zero.

## Capacitance
The amount of charge $Q$ that a capacitor can store for a given value of p.d $V$ is the capacitance. 

Therefore, the capacitance of a capacitor is defined as the charge stored per unit p.d across it:
$$
C = \frac{Q}{V}
$$
Where $C$ is the capacitance in farads $(F)$, Q is charge stored and $V$ is potential difference across the capacitor. Thus the charge is:
$$
Q=CV
$$
For any capacitor, the greater the amount of positive and negative charge stored in the two plates, the greater the p.d across them:
$$
Q \propto V
$$
The unit Farad $1 F =  1$ coulomb per volt $CV^{-1}$.
