In medicine radioactive isotopes are used in diagnosis and treatment. They are used in CAT scanners to identify abnormalities without surgery. 

Another diagnostic tool is the gamma camera. This is a detector of radioactive gamma photons from radioactive nuclei injected into the patient. 

## Choosing radioisotopes heading and weight

Gamma-emitting sources are ideal as gamma photons are the least ionising and penetrate through the patient. 

Radioisotopes chosen for this must have a short half-life to ensure high activity. This is to ensure that only a small amount is required to form the image and the patient does not have prolonged exposure. 

Fluorine-18 has short half life and is produced using an on-site particle accelerator.  Technetium-99m (Tc-99m) is another versatile candidate. It is produced in the natural decay of Molybdenum-99 (Mo-99). 

Mo-99 decays beta-minus with a half life of 67 hours. The daughter Tc-99m is metastable. This means the nucleus is in a greater energy state than the stable nucleus for a longer period than expected. 

The Tc-99m isotope has a half-life of 6 hours gamma decays by emitting a gamma photon of energy $140keV$. A stable Tc-99 remains with half-life of 210 000 years.

$$
^{99}_{42}Mo \to ^{99m}_{43} Tc + ^{0}_{-1}e + \bar{v_{e}} \to ^{99}_{43}Tc + \gamma
$$
# Medical tracers

Medical tracers, which are referred to as radiopharmacetuicals, are chemically combined with elements that target desired tissues. For example Tc-99m can be chemically combined with sodium and oxygen to make $NaTcO_{4}$.  This compound is injected into the bloodstream and target the cells in the brain. The compound is traced in the body as $^{99m}_{43}Tc$ emits gamma photons. 

## Use of the gamma camera
The gamma cameras detects the photons as the medical tracer travels through the patient. These photons travel towards a collimater, a lead honeycomb of long, thin tubes. Any photons arriving at angle relative to the tubes are absorbed. Only photons aligned with axis of the tubes reach the scintillator. 

The scintillator is made of sodium iodide. When a gamma photon strikes it, thousands of photons of visible light are produced. There is 1 in 10 chance of a gamma photon to to strike the scintillator.

These photons of visible light travel into hexagonally arranged photomutliplier tubes. Each photon is converted into an electrical pulse and fed into a computer. Sophisticated software then processes the electrical signals to construct a high-quality colour map of the concentrations of the medical traver in the patients body. This image can help identify activity and infected areas.

# Photomultipliers 

Below is a simple diagram of a photomultiplier. 
![[Pasted image 20260318072532.png]]

A single photon of visible light hits the photocathode and produces a photoelectron. This photon electron is then accelerated to the first electrode which is held at a potential of +100V. The high speed impact of this electron produces an average of four secondary electrons. These secondary electrons are accelerated towards a second electrode of +200V, in turn creating four secondary electrons for each electron. This process is repeated at successive dynodes, with the number of photoelectrodes growing exponentially at rate $4^{n}$, where $n$ is the $nth$ dynode. 

There must be increasing voltage as there must be a change in voltage for there to be an acceleration of electrons.
