Whenever a **wave source**  moves relative to an observer, the frequency and wavelength of the waves received by the observer change compared with what would be observed without relative motion.

# Doppler Shift
Consider a wave source that is stationary to two observed at $A$ and $B$. Both observed experience waves at the same frequency and wavelength $\lambda$ as they were emitted from the source:

However if you were to consider a second scenario in which the save source is moving away from observed $C$ and towards observer $D$. The waves received by D will be compressed and $\lambda_{D}$ will be shorter. and so a higher frequency is observed as $f\propto \frac{1}{\lambda}$ The waves received by $C$ will be elongated and $\lambda_{C}$ will be longer and subsequently a higher frequency.

# Doppler shifts in starlight
Light from stars can be analysed using spectra from stars. The Doppler effect can be used to determine the relative velocity of a distant galaxy. 

First, the absoption spectrum of a specific element in determined in the laboratory. The same spectrum is observed in light from a distant galaxy. The difference in the observed wavelengths of the absorption lines must be caused by the relative motion between the galaxy and the Earth.

- Any galaxy moving towards the Earth will have the wavelengths of light shifted towards the the blue end of the spectrum
- If the galaxy is moving away from the Earth, the absorption lines will be red-shifted, the will shift towards the red end of the spectrum.

# Doppler equation
How fast the wave source moves relative to the observe affects the size of the observed shift in wavelength and frequency. 

The magnitude of the relative velocity between the source and the observer can be expressed in this equation:
$$
\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{\Delta f}{f} \approx \frac{v}{c}
$$
This shows that the faster the source moves, the greater the observed change in wavelength and frequency. 

With spectra lines, the wavelength of each absorption lines changes by the same percentage for a particular galaxy. 

# Worked Example 1
In a laboratory an absorption line of hydrogen is observed at a wavelength of $656.4nm$. In a distant galaxy, the same absorption line observed at $658.1nm$. Calculate the speed of the galaxy and state whether it's moving towards or away from the Earth.

1. Calculate the change in wavelength:
$$
\Delta \lambda = 658.1-656.4 = 1.7nm
$$
As there is a positive increase in wavelength from the wavelength observed in the galaxy than the laboratory, the galaxy must be receding. This means the spectral line has been red-shifted.

2. Substituting the values into the Doppler equation to find the relative velocity of the galaxy, relative to the laboratory. 
$$
\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx \frac{v}{c}
$$
We can rearrange to get v as:
$$
\frac{c\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} \approx v
$$
Substituting the values of the wavelength and using $c=3\times 10^{8}$:
$$
v = \frac{c\Delta\lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{(3\times 10^{8})(1.7\times 10 ^{-9})}{656.4\times 10 ^{-9}} = 7.76\dots \times 10^{5}ms^{-1} = 780kms ^{-1}
$$
