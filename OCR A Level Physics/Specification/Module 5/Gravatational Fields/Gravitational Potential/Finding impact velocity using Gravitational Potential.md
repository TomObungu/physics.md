To calculate the velocity of a test mass in a gravitational field of an object with mass $M$. You must equate kinetic energy and the gravitational potential of the object using the gravitational potential as $mV_{g}$


## Calculating velocity using changes in $Vg$
Often the impact velocity of a test mass will be at the surface of the planet with mass $M$. The distance from centre of the planet to its surface is usually given as $r_{1}$ and the object is a distance $r_{2}$ away from  the centre of the planet. 

The change in gravitational potential $\Delta Vg$  for different radii, $r_{1}$ and $r_{2}$ will be the difference in the reciprocals of the the radii. This will be the case as a small mass moves from $r_{1}$ to $r_{2}$:
$$
\begin{gather*}
\Delta Vg = -\frac{GM}{r_{2}}-\left( -\frac{GM}{r_{1}} \right) \\ \\
\therefore \boxed{ \Delta Vg = GM\left( \frac{1}{r_{1}} - \frac{1}{r_{2}} \right)}
\end{gather*}
$$


## Finding velocity using change in GPE
The kinetic energy gained by the test mass will be equal to the change in GPE or $mV_{g}$
$$
\frac{1}{2}mv^{2} = m\Delta V_{g}
$$
However as the mass is on both sides, it is possible to divide the mass and cancel it out.

From this we can deduce that the kinetic energy gained for any object is in a gravitational field of point mass $M$ is:
$$
\boxed{\frac{1}{2}v^{2} = \Delta V_{g}}
$$

### Why this works
If you consider a familiar equation $\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}=mgh$. This only works for the case when our gravitational field is our Earth's. However using Newton's laws of gravitation we can apply the same concept with different planets

On Earth, $gh$ is to the gravitational potential energy multiplied by another distance value. If you look at the Newtonian equivalent calculations:
$$
\begin{gather*}
g = -\frac{GM}{r^{2}}
\end{gather*}
$$
The value $h$ is just additional value of $r$ so multiplying by $r$ has the same effect of removing the square power of $r$ in the denominator, leading to:
$$
gh = -\frac{GM}{r^{2}}r = -\frac{GM}{r}
$$
Which is the exact equation for $Vg$ therefore we can see that $mgh$ is just $mV_{g}$  abstracted for the case of Earth.

This is why it the text book recommends to just use gravitational potential and the radius from the centre plus any additional value of height all in one equation as $mVg$ instead of calculating the value of g for the planet and then using $mgh$.  

In essence, $mV_{g}$ is just $mgh$ but you for any planet with a different gravitational field strength.
