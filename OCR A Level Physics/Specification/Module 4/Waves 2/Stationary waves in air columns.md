## Stationary waves in a tube closed at one end
In order for a stationary tube to be formed in this scenario, there needs to be a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end. Recall that the distance between a node and an adjacent antinode is $\frac{\lambda}{4}$. 
![[Pasted image 20260501142034.png]]

Unlike stationary waves on a string stationary waves with one end closed cannot have a node at it's end. This means there are no even harmonics of fundamental frequency that is even. 

Therefore the frequencies of harmonics in tubes closed at one end are always an odd multiple of the fundamental frequency:
$$
f_{0}, 3f_{0}, 5f_{0}, 7f_{0}\dots
$$
Thus the corresponding waves lengths will be:
$$
\frac{1\lambda}{4},\frac{3\lambda}{4}, \frac{5\lambda}{4}\dots
$$
And so on. 


## Speed of sound in a resonance tube
Holding a vibrating tuning fork above a resonance tube will cause the air in the tube to vibrate. If the air in the tube is resonating, the air in the tube is oscillating at a fundamental frequency. This is distinguished by the sound of the tuning fork becoming noticeably amplified. 
![[Pasted image 20260501141507.png]]

As this setup models a stationary tube with a closed end, the wavelength of the stationary wave will be $\frac{\lambda}{4}$. Therefore the length of the tube that's above water, $L$, will be equal to the wavelength of sound. Therefore the wavelength of sound can be calculated to be:
$$
\lambda = 4L
$$

This experiment can be modified to consider different frequency pitch forks. This can be done by varying the length of the tube in the water until there is resonance in the tube. 

## Stationary waves in open tubes
For a tube open at both ends, there must be an antinode at both ends. Therefore the total length of the wave will be $\frac{\lambda}{2}.$
![[Pasted image 20260501142042.png]]
Therefore, unlike a tube closed at end, harmonics can take place at all integer multiples of fundamental frequency.
$$
f_{0}, 2f_{0}
$$
